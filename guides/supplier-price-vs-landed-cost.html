Supplier Price vs Landed Cost: Why the Cheapest Quote Can Cost More

A quoted unit price is only one layer of an import order. The comparison becomes useful when every quote is normalized to the same destination and cost scope.

Quick answer

Supplier price answers “what the goods cost.” Landed cost answers “what the goods cost at your chosen destination.”

Two suppliers can reverse positions after freight, insurance, duty, clearance and delivery are added. That is why price comparisons should use the same quantity, currency, specification and destination.

Worked example

The cost stack

What sits between quote price and landed cost?

01

Supplier purchase cost

Unit price multiplied by order quantity, plus directly attributable setup, tooling or packaging charges.

02

Origin and international logistics

Pickup, export handling, international freight and insurance depend heavily on Incoterm and shipment method.

03

Border costs

Duty, import tax where applicable, brokerage and clearance charges can materially affect the unit economics.

04

Destination costs

Port or terminal charges, inland delivery and other direct costs should be included up to the same destination for every supplier.

Normalize before deciding

Use the same commercial basis.

Match specification, currency, quantity and destination first. Then identify which costs are already included in each quote. Do not add the same freight or insurance twice, and do not assume CIF, FOB or DDP descriptions are interchangeable.

Beyond cost

Landed cost is important, but it is not the entire supplier decision.

Lead time, MOQ, payment terms, specification consistency, quality history and supplier reliability can outweigh a small cost difference. ImportMetric's quote tool deliberately shows numbers rather than producing a buy/reject recommendation.
